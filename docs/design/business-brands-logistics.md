# Business, Brands, and Logistics

## Playable business ownership

A player may control a mortal actor who owns shares, voting rights, debt claims, board seats, or a privately held firm. Ownership, control, management, and beneficial income are separate. A minority owner can influence but not command; a founder can lose economic ownership yet retain voting control; creditors and governments can impose covenants.

Business gameplay must expose real decisions: product portfolio, capacity, technology, sourcing, inventory policy, wages, hiring, financing, dividends, reinvestment, location, logistics contracts, pricing, quality, distribution, marketing, lobbying, compliance, mergers, and succession. The player receives only information available to the actor and firm.

## Governance

Firms have legal form, shareholders, voting classes, board, executives, delegated authority, contracts, and reporting quality. Decisions require the appropriate authority. Fraud, principal-agent conflict, nepotism, hostile takeovers, bankruptcy, nationalization, and inheritance emerge from these structures.

## Competition and product choice

Consumers do not choose the globally most popular producer. Each offer is evaluated by an audience and purchase context using price, expected quality, availability, delivery time, habit, compatibility, switching cost, warranty/service, source trust, identity fit, ethics, status, advertising exposure, peer use, and past experience.

Brand state is audience-specific and multidimensional: awareness, expected quality, trust, familiarity, status, identity association, service reputation, and remembered incidents. Marketing buys reach, placement, trials, sponsorship, distribution support, and message production; it does not directly add popularity. Claims flow through the same information/propaganda system as politics and can mislead, backfire, or create short-term sales followed by reputation damage.

## Logistics

Goods move through explicit routes, inventories, vehicles, terminals, warehouses, customs, contracts, and capacity. Cost, delay, reliability, spoilage, theft, congestion, sanctions, weather, and war matter. Firms choose own-account logistics, spot freight, or long-term contracts.

Logistics companies should exist as ordinary playable firms, not a special exception. Their products are transport and warehousing services; their capital is fleets, depots, terminals, software, and route rights. They buy fuel, labor, maintenance, insurance, and finance. This allows freight booms, bankruptcies, monopolies, strikes, blockades, and vertical integration to emerge naturally.

## Implementation slices

1. Ownership stakes, voting control, boards, and management authority.
2. Firm policy commands through the same actor-command boundary used by AI.
3. Product offers, audience-specific brand beliefs, and repeat-purchase memory.
4. Warehouses, route graph, shipment orders, carrier capacity, cost, delay, reliability.
5. Logistics firms and freight contracts.
6. Advertising campaigns linked to claims, channels, audiences, sales evidence, and source credibility.
7. Financing, accounting statements, insolvency, takeover, inheritance, and player succession.

## Acceptance patterns

- A cheaper unknown product can beat a trusted brand for price-sensitive households but lose among risk-sensitive buyers.
- Advertising can raise awareness without raising trust or sales.
- Repeated stockouts damage retailer and brand reliability.
- A distant efficient producer can lose to a local inefficient producer because freight is constrained.
- A logistics firm can become politically important by controlling a bottleneck.
- An owner can be unable to execute a decision because board, creditors, law, managers, or workers resist.
