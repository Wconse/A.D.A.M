# Ideologies, Institutions, and Regimes

## Principle

Neither ideology nor form of government is a single enum that dictates behavior. Named ideologies and regime labels are human-readable classifications over underlying beliefs, institutions, coalitions, and enforcement capacity.

## Ideological dimensions

Actors, organizations, and population cohorts hold positions with different salience and certainty across dimensions:

- ownership: private, cooperative, state, communal;
- distribution: egalitarian to strongly hierarchical;
- markets: laissez-faire to comprehensive planning;
- labor: employer autonomy to worker control;
- welfare: minimal responsibility to universal provision;
- political participation: restricted to universal;
- executive authority: dispersed to concentrated;
- civil liberty versus security and order;
- centralization versus regional autonomy;
- nationalism, civic patriotism, imperialism, and cosmopolitanism;
- secularism, pluralism, and religious authority;
- traditionalism versus social transformation;
- militarism, pacifism, and doctrines of legitimate force;
- technocracy versus popular or traditional authority;
- environmental extraction versus stewardship;
- international alignment, sovereignty, and integration;
- attitudes toward migration and citizenship;
- information freedom, censorship, and epistemic authority.

Beliefs also have priority, rigidity, and acceptable compromise. Two actors can agree on policy for different ideological reasons.

## Named traditions

Named traditions are data-driven clusters and historical lineages, not hard-coded behavior classes. The initial library should cover families such as:

- liberalism: classical, social, conservative, national;
- conservatism: traditional, paternalistic, fiscal, national;
- socialism: democratic, revolutionary, market, state-planned;
- social democracy and laborism;
- communism and its competing institutional traditions;
- anarchism: individualist, mutualist, syndicalist, communist;
- nationalism: civic, ethnic, expansionist, anti-colonial;
- fascist and ultranationalist movements;
- religious democracy, clericalism, theocracy, and religious socialism;
- monarchism: constitutional, traditional, absolutist;
- libertarianism and minarchism;
- technocracy and managerial developmentalism;
- agrarianism, green politics, and eco-authoritarianism;
- populism as a political frame that can combine with left, right, religious, or regional programs;
- military corporatism and security-state doctrines;
- regionalism, federalism, separatism, and indigenous sovereignty movements.

The list is extensible in data. Real actors may be syncretic, inconsistent, opportunistic, or ideologically indifferent.

## Belief formation and change

Ideology changes through:

- family and education;
- workplace and class position;
- religious and civic organizations;
- personal gains and losses;
- war, crisis, migration, and repression;
- elite persuasion and propaganda;
- peer networks;
- policy performance;
- generational replacement.

Actors can strategically advocate a position they do not privately hold. Public platform, private belief, and enacted policy are separate.

## Regime decomposition

A regime is constructed from institutions:

- source of legitimacy;
- head-of-state selection and succession;
- executive formation and removal;
- legislature structure and authority;
- franchise and candidate access;
- party legality and party-system competitiveness;
- judicial independence and constitutional review;
- federal, unitary, autonomous, or fragmented territorial order;
- civil-service professionalism and patronage;
- civil-military command and coup capacity;
- media ownership, censorship, and surveillance;
- property and labor institutions;
- emergency powers;
- local-government autonomy;
- real enforcement and tax capacity.

A written constitution can differ from actual practice. De jure and de facto institutions are tracked separately.

## Recognizable regime families

Human-readable labels may include:

- parliamentary, presidential, semi-presidential, and assembly democracies;
- constitutional and absolute monarchies;
- dominant-party and one-party states;
- personalist dictatorships;
- military juntas and military tutelage;
- oligarchic and plutocratic republics;
- theocracies and clerical guardianship systems;
- technocratic or bureaucratic authoritarian systems;
- council, communal, and confederal systems;
- competitive authoritarian and other hybrid regimes;
- colonial, occupation, and protectorate administrations;
- fragmented, warlord, dual-power, and failed-state conditions.

These labels are derived summaries. A country can transition gradually, occupy several categories, or have different regimes in different regions.

## Political process

Policy requires a proposal, agenda access, coalition formation, institutional passage, implementation resources, bureaucratic compliance, local compliance, and survival against legal or extra-legal opposition. Failure at each stage produces a different event and political consequence.

Coalitions trade policy concessions, appointments, budgets, contracts, protection, information, public endorsements, and credible future commitments. Loyalty is not guaranteed by a single payment.

## Regime change

Regime change can emerge through election, constitutional reform, executive aggrandizement, coup, revolution, civil war, occupation, negotiated transition, state collapse, or incremental institutional decay. Success depends on control of real institutions and organizations, not a scripted ideology event.

## Minimal implementation slice

1. Define offices, appointment rules, veto points, and implementation capacity.
2. Give actors private preferences, public platforms, ambition, risk tolerance, and obligations.
3. Resolve one distributive policy through coalition bargaining.
4. Apply the policy to households and firms.
5. Feed material outcomes into reputation and coalition stability.
6. Allow institutional practice to diverge from formal law.
